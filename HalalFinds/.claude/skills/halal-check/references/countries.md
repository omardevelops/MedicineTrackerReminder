# Country and certification reference

Why the country matters: the same product is not equally *knowable* in every
market. Labelling law decides how much the pack must disclose, and national
standards decide which additives a certifier will accept.

## Numbering systems

| System | Where | On the label |
|---|---|---|
| E-numbers | EU, UK, Gulf, Malaysia, Türkiye, Pakistan, South Africa | `E471`, often with a class name: `emulsifier (E471)` |
| INS numbers | Australia, New Zealand, India, Codex | Bare: `emulsifier (471)` — identical meaning, no `E` |
| Common names | US, Canada | `mono- and diglycerides` — expect no numbers at all |

A US label will not print `E471`. Searching a US panel for E-numbers and
finding none means nothing.

## Jurisdictions

### European Union

Regulation 1169/2011: additives declared by functional class plus name or
E-number; the 14 regulated allergens emphasised in the ingredient list.

The allergen rule is quietly useful. Fish is a declarable allergen, so gelatine
with no fish allergen flag is very likely bovine or porcine. Annex VI also
requires the species to be named for added meat.

"Natural flavouring" remains permitted without source disclosure, so it stays
doubtful here as everywhere.

### United Kingdom

Retained EU law, so the same rules apply. Two main certifiers with different
standards: **HFA** accepts mechanically stunned poultry, **HMC** does not. A
user following HMC will get a different answer on certified meat than one
following HFA.

### United States

FDA labelling uses common names, not E-numbers.

The important asymmetry: 21 CFR 101.22 permits "natural flavors" to include
material derived from meat, dairy, eggs or seafood with no further disclosure.
"Spices" and "flavors" may lawfully conceal individual components. So a US
label is *less resolvable* than an EU one, and this system escalates natural
flavouring and sugar accordingly.

Working the other way: carmine and cochineal extract must be declared by name
(21 CFR 73.100), so undeclared carmine is unlikely in the US.

Cane sugar may be bone-char refined; beet sugar never is.

### Malaysia

MS 1500 is the national standard; the **JAKIM** logo is authoritative and
legally protected. JAKIM rejects carmine and wine vinegar, so it is stricter
than the mainstream default on those.

A valid JAKIM mark means the product has been audited — no ingredient-level
analysis is needed.

### Indonesia

Law 33/2014 makes halal certification mandatory for food in circulation.
**BPJPH** issues certificates on **MUI** fatwa. Non-halal products must be
explicitly labelled as such, which makes the Indonesian market unusually
legible. MUI treats carmine as impermissible.

### Gulf states

**UAE**: GSO 2055-1 is the standard; the National Halal Mark is administered by
MOIAT. Arabic labelling is mandatory, so ingredient names may appear only in
Arabic — transcribe them as printed.

**Saudi Arabia**: SFDA enforces GSO requirements on imports. Pork and alcohol
cannot be legally imported, so declared pork is very unlikely on a product
lawfully on sale. Defaults to the `strict` profile.

### India

FSSAI mandates a **green dot** for vegetarian products and a brown/red dot for
non-vegetarian ones. This is the single most useful label mark in any market
covered here: a green dot rules out meat, fish and egg, which resolves
animal-source doubt on E471, glycerol, gelatine and most emulsifiers in one
step.

It does not resolve alcohol-carrier doubt, and it does not resolve carmine.

### Australia and New Zealand

FSANZ uses bare INS numbers. `emulsifier (471)` is E471.

### Türkiye

**HAK** is the national accreditation authority; OIC/SMIIC 1 is the reference
standard. GIMDES also certifies.

### Singapore

**MUIS** is the sole certification authority and its mark is legally protected.

### South Africa

Several recognised bodies; **SANHA** and **MJC** marks are the most common.

## On certification marks

A valid mark from a recognised body outranks any ingredient-label analysis,
because the certifier has audited the supply chain — including processing aids
and cross-contamination that never appear on a label.

Two caveats worth stating to a user: check the mark is current, and check the
body is one they recognise. Self-declared "halal" text with no certifying body
named is not certification. In Canada this is regulated — an SFCR halal claim
must name the certifying body next to it.
